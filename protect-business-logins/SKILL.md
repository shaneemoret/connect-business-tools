# Protect My Business Logins

Acceptance: the owner ends with every business login saved in one password manager, two-step verification confirmed on, off or unknown for each tool (never guessed), each one that was off or unknown walked through to on, and one plain table they can keep. No password, code or recovery key ever passes through the chat.

The owner runs a business; they are not an engineer. Talk in plain words, one line per screen. Do not ask them anything you can find out yourself from their email or this computer. The only steps that are theirs are the ones that need their hands: typing a master password, a password or a code, scanning a code with their phone, and pressing Save.

## Hard limits

These are "may not", not "ask first".

1. You may not ask for, read out, type, copy or store any password, master password, two-step code, recovery code or recovery key. If the owner pastes one into the chat by mistake, tell them it is exposed and walk them through changing it.
2. You may not change, move, export or delete any saved password, and may not turn two-step verification off anywhere.
3. You may not create accounts, buy anything or start a paid plan. Suggesting a paid manager is fine; the owner signs up themselves.
4. You may not send an email or message to anyone. Email is read only.
5. LinkedIn is excluded. Do not open, check or automate it, and leave it out of the table.

## 1. Find the password manager

Look on this computer and in the owner's email for: Bitwarden, 1Password, the Passwords app or Keychain on a Mac, passwords saved in Google Chrome or Google Password Manager, Microsoft Edge or their Microsoft account, and any other manager's welcome, receipt or sign-in emails.

- If they already use one, keep it. Do not suggest moving.
- If they have none, suggest the best one for them in one line with the reason, based on their computer (Mac or Windows), team size from their email, and the browser they use. Bitwarden and 1Password are the most common paid choices. If they don't want to pay, use a free one: Google's, Microsoft's, or the one built into their computer.
- Walk them through setting it up in their own browser, one screen at a time. They choose and type their own master password.

## 2. List the business tools they sign in to

Search the last two months of their business email for companies, not people: receipts, welcome and verify emails, sign-in alerts, two-step codes and account notices. One row per product. Leave out personal shopping, streaming, utilities and banking that is not the business's. For each tool, check whether its login is saved in the manager (by the name of the entry only; never open or read a password). Mark saved: yes or no.

## 3. Check two-step verification on each tool

Evidence first:
- Emails that show it is on: two-step codes, "two-step verification turned on", "new sign-in, verified with your phone".
- Then open the tool's own security settings page in the owner's browser to confirm, looking only.

Mark each tool on, off or unknown. Unknown means you could not confirm it; never write off when you only lack proof.

For every tool that is off or unknown, open its security page and walk the owner through turning it on, one screen at a time. Prefer an authenticator app or a passkey over text messages where the tool offers both. The owner scans the code and presses Save. Remind them to store the recovery codes in their password manager, never in the chat.

## 4. Finish

Show one plain table: tool, login saved (yes or no), two-step verification (on, off or unknown), what is left to do. Save it as `logins-and-2fa.md` in a folder for the business on this computer, with tool names only, never a password or code. End with one line: what is done and what is left.

If a tool, page or browser step fails, fixing it is the job: read the exact error, search the tool's own help pages, apply the fix, try again, and keep going. Stop only for a step that needs the owner's hands.
